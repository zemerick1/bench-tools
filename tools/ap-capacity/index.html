AP Capacity

“How many users can this access point support?” is a sales question with an engineering answer. This tab does the engineering answer: if you set these things, you can expect about this many people, at about this much each — not the PHY rate on the box.

What this is: a practical airtime budget. You pick the AP (radios, generation, width, streams), who is connecting, what they need to do, how noisy the neighbors are, and how many SSIDs you insisted on broadcasting. It uses IEEE MCS rates, then derates them the way a real BSS behaves.

What this is not: a coverage survey, a channel plan, a promise, or permission to quote 4.8 Gbps in a meeting. Half-duplex. The slowest active client sets the airtime price. Associated-but-idle does not count as a user here.
Stays in this tab. No upload, no “AI RF design,” no SKU database. Device names are borrowed from common client presets (phones, laptops, IoT) so you are not starting from a blank MCS index.
Start from a story

1. What are people doing?

Active transmitters, not badge-in headcount. One person with a paused YouTube tab is not a user for this math.

2. The access point

20 MHz is the honest enterprise default. Widen a channel only if that spectrum is actually empty. Widths and bands that the generation cannot do (Wi-Fi 4 at 160 MHz, 6 GHz on ac, and so on) are not offered.

3. Who is connecting?

The AP and the client negotiate the worse of the two. A Wi-Fi 7 phone on a Wi-Fi 6 AP is a Wi-Fi 6 phone. IoT that only does 2.4 GHz never sees the 6 GHz radio.

Link quality

Typical coverage is 64-QAM / 256-QAM. 1024-QAM and 4096-QAM are “I am sitting under the AP.”

4. Assumptions we will not hide

Change these if your room is a single AP in a faraday cage, or a 2.4 GHz war zone. Default is a normal floor.

Neighboring APs (CCI / ACI)

Isolated = 100% of the channel is yours. Typical = 60% yours. Crowded = 40% yours.

Beacons and probes are not free. They are especially not free on 2.4 GHz at 1 Mbps.

Where clients land

Band-steered: a little leftover on 2.4, the rest on 5/6 if the device can. “Best radio” piles everyone onto the highest band they speak.

FAQ

Why isn’t this the gigabit number on the datasheet?

That number is PHY rate — layer 1, before Wi-Fi overhead. A single client under ideal RF might see 50–70% of PHY as TCP throughput. A room full of active clients is closer to 40%. Then neighbors, extra SSIDs, and 1-stream stations take another bite. We never quote PHY as user speed.

Where do the 50% / 45% / 40% numbers come from?

Protocol overhead: IFS, ACKs, backoff, retries, small frames. One active client on a clean radio can see about 50% of PHY as application throughput. A handful of clients drops that toward 45%. A busy room (a dozen or more active) is about 40%. Example: 30× 1-stream 11n clients at 72 Mbps PHY × 40% ≈ 29 Mbps total ≈ 950 kbps each — not enough for 2 Mbps unicast video. Same room, 2-stream 11ac at 173 Mbps × 40% ≈ 2.3 Mbps each. One 3-stream laptop left alone: 289 × 50% ≈ 145 Mbps. The math is boring on purpose.

What does “60% of the channel is yours” mean?

Wi-Fi is a shared half-duplex medium. Neighboring APs on the same or overlapping channel (CCI / ACI) burn airtime you do not get. Isolated is the classroom with no other BSS — 100% of the leftover airtime after protocol overhead. Typical assumes about 60% remains yours. Crowded is 40%, and 2.4 GHz often lives there even when 5/6 look fine. This is not a substitute for looking at channel utilization on a survey.

Why do extra SSIDs hurt?

Every SSID sends beacons, usually at the lowest basic rate (often 1 Mbps on 2.4, 6 Mbps on 5/6), plus probe responses. Four SSIDs is a lot. Eight is how you donate the 2.4 GHz radio to management frames. We subtract that tax before we talk about user traffic.

QAM, MCS, spatial streams — do I have to care?

Only if you want to. MCS is a gear number: modulation (QPSK, 16-QAM, 64-QAM, 256-QAM, 1024-QAM, 4096-QAM) plus a coding rate. Higher QAM packs more bits per symbol and needs a cleaner signal. Spatial streams are how many parallel radios the client actually has — most phones are 2SS, a pile of IoT is 1SS. We pick MCS from link quality unless you override it. The answer is still “about N people at X Mbps.”

Does OFDMA / MU-MIMO 4× the number?

No. OFDMA helps when many clients send small frames at once. It does not turn a 20 MHz radio into four 20 MHz radios. MU-MIMO in the field has been underwhelming for the same reason most rooms are not a lab demo. We do not bake a marketing multiplier into the total.

Is this a coverage or channel plan?

No. It does not place APs, pick channels, or know your walls. If MCS is low with strong RSSI, that is interference, not distance. If MCS is low with weak RSSI, add an AP. This tab will not walk the floor for you.

Where do the data rates come from?

PHY rates follow IEEE 802.11 (n/ac/ax/be): channel width, streams, MCS/QAM, and guard interval. Device presets are common phones, laptops, and IoT — not a vendor SKU list. Real speed is still less than the datasheet number.
