AP Capacity

“How many users can this access point support?” does not have one engineering answer. This tab compares airtime stories. You set the radios, one kind of device, a megabit target, a band split, and a rough guess at the neighbors. It shows what that story produces. It is not a survey, and it is not a headcount to build to.

What this is: an airtime scenario estimator. IEEE MCS rates, then a few coarse assumptions: protocol overhead, how much of the channel this story keeps, and where people land. The card lists each radio, names the one that runs out first, and shows a modeled maximum with no spare airtime plus a smaller recommended count.

What this is not: a coverage survey, a channel plan, a promise, or permission to quote the PHY rate in a meeting. Half-duplex. Associated-but-idle does not count as a user here.

The real answer is “it depends.” A Wi-Fi cell is a shared, contention-based radio, and most of what decides it is not on this form. People across the room do not hold one MCS. The rate shifts, and uplink often negotiates worse than downlink. 2.4, 5, and 6 GHz do not share interference, contention, or how far the signal goes. 2.4 GHz is often just crowded. A real room is not one laptop: mixed generations, streams, channel widths, and apps share the air, and the slow devices spend more of it. A call can fail on delay, jitter, and loss while a stream still looks fine, because calls need both directions. Where you mount the AP, and what the neighbors are doing right now, changes the result. That is a measurement, not a preset. Use this page to compare stories. Do not treat the number as the design.
Stays in this tab. No upload, no “AI RF design.” Aruba campus and Juniper Mist APs are a starting point for radios and streams. Channel width, SSIDs, and RF stay yours. Device names are common phones, laptops, and IoT so you are not starting from a blank MCS index.
Start from a story

1. What are people doing?

Active transmitters, not badge-in headcount. One person with a paused YouTube tab is not a user for this math.

2. The access point

Pick an Aruba or Juniper Mist AP to fill radios, generation, and streams — or leave Custom and set them yourself. 20 MHz is the honest enterprise default. Widen a channel only if that spectrum is actually empty. Widths and bands that the generation cannot do (Wi-Fi 4 at 160 MHz, 6 GHz on ac, and so on) are not offered.

Radios
Generation

Applies to every radio. Mixed-stream APs (515, 555, 745) set this per radio instead. Changing this box forces all radios to match.

3. Who is connecting?

The AP and the client negotiate the worse of the two. A Wi-Fi 7 phone on a Wi-Fi 6 AP is a Wi-Fi 6 phone. IoT that only does 2.4 GHz never sees the 6 GHz radio. This story is one kind of device for the whole room. A real room is mixed, and the slow devices spend more airtime.

Link quality

Typical coverage is 64-QAM / 256-QAM. 1024-QAM and 4096-QAM are “I am sitting under the AP.” One MCS stands for every client on that radio, same rate up and down. Real cells rate-shift, and uplink is often worse.

4. Assumptions we will not hide

These are coarse assumptions for comparing stories, not measurements from your floor. Change them to see the story move. They will not become a survey.

Neighboring APs (CCI / ACI)

Isolated = 100% of the channel is yours. Typical = 60% yours. Crowded = 40% yours. Until you open “Bands differ,” every band uses this same guess. 2.4 GHz is often worse than 5 and 6.

Bands differ

2.4 GHz is often crowded when 5 and 6 are not. Opening this does not change the numbers. Change a band when you want the story to say so.

Beacons and probes are not free. They are especially not free on 2.4 GHz at 1 Mbps.

Where clients land

Band-steered leaves a share on 2.4 and puts the rest on the higher bands the device can use. That share is an assumption, not a count of who associated. “Best radio” puts everyone on the highest band they speak.

Percent, not a measurement. The rest splits across the higher bands in this story. One device still uses only one radio.

Spare airtime

The modeled maximum fills this story. Wi-Fi does not run well full. Recommended leaves this much unused.

Force an MCS index

Same idea as an MCS table: higher is faster and needs a cleaner signal. Invalid combos step down (MCS 9 at 20 MHz on most 802.11ac links is N/A).

Glossary

The words this tab uses, once, in plain language. Nothing here changes the math.

PHY, QAM, CCI, SSIDs, and the rest
ACK
Acknowledgement. After a data frame the receiver says “got it.” That reply uses airtime, which is why a one-way video stream still spends time talking back.
ACI
Adjacent-channel interference. A neighbor on a nearby channel whose energy leaks into yours. You still lose airtime; this tab folds it in with CCI on the neighbor slider.
Airtime
The clock on a shared radio. Only one station talks at a time. Slow clients, extra SSIDs, and neighbors all buy time you do not get for payload.
Beacon
The “I am here” frame every SSID sends, usually at the slowest basic rate. Cheap per frame, expensive in a pile — especially on 2.4 GHz at 1 Mbps.
BSS
Basic service set: this AP plus the clients associated to it, on one channel. Isolated-cell math assumes you are the only BSS on that channel.
CCI
Co-channel interference. Another AP (or a pile of them) on the same channel. You wait your turn. Typical floor: we assume you keep about 60% of the channel.
Channel width
How wide a slice of spectrum the radio uses (20 / 40 / 80 / 160 / 320 MHz). Wider is faster on a clean slice and ruder on a busy floor. 20 MHz is the honest enterprise default here.
Guard interval GI
A short gap between symbols so echoes do not smear the next one. Shorter is a bit faster and a bit more fragile. We use the generation’s usual value; you do not have to pick it.
Half-duplex
The radio cannot send and receive user data at the same time. Datasheet gigabits are one-way PHY. Real TCP is a conversation.
IFS
Interframe space. Mandatory silence between frames so the medium can settle. It is not payload. It is why “protocol efficiency” is never 100%.
MCS
Modulation and coding scheme. A gear number: which QAM, which coding rate, how many bits per symbol. Higher is faster and needs a cleaner signal. This tab picks MCS from link quality unless you override it.
MU-MIMO
Multi-user MIMO: talk to more than one client on different streams at once. Lab demos look great. Field results have been underwhelming. We do not multiply the total by four.
OFDMA
Split one transmission across several clients with small frames. Helps dense uplink chatter. Does not turn a 20 MHz radio into four 20 MHz radios.
PHY
Physical-layer rate. The number on the box. Bits on the air before Wi-Fi overhead, neighbors, or TCP. We never quote it as user speed.
Probe
“Is there a network named X?” Clients shout this; APs answer. Extra SSIDs mean extra answers. Counted with beacons in the SSID tax.
QAM
Quadrature amplitude modulation. How many bits you pack in one symbol (16, 64, 256, 1024, 4096). Higher QAM needs to be next to the AP. Typical coverage here is 64-QAM on 2.4 and 256-QAM on 5/6.
Radio
One concurrent transmitter in the AP (2.4, 5, or 6 GHz). Dual-radio is two at once. Tri-radio is three. A dual-radio tri-band AP (615) still only runs two at a time.
RSSI
Received signal strength. Strong RSSI with a low MCS is interference. Weak RSSI with a low MCS is distance — add an AP.
Spatial streams NSS, SS, 2×2
Parallel radios on one link (1×1, 2×2, 4×4). The AP and the client use the worse of the two. Buying 8×8 does not make a 2-stream phone faster.
Modeled maximum
How many people this story can hold before the tight radio misses the megabit target, with the split you picked and no spare airtime. It is the model’s ceiling, not a design.
Recommended
The modeled maximum with your safety margin left unused. Default is 30% unused. Wi-Fi does not run well full.
SSID
The network name you broadcast. Each one sends beacons and answers probes. One is already inside the protocol factor. Extra ones are a tax, especially on 2.4 GHz.

FAQ

Why isn’t this the gigabit number on the datasheet?

That number is PHY rate — layer 1, before Wi-Fi overhead. A single client under ideal RF might see 50–70% of PHY as TCP throughput. A room full of active clients is closer to 40%. Then neighbors, extra SSIDs, and 1-stream stations take another bite. We never quote PHY as user speed.

Where do the 50% / 45% / 40% numbers come from?

Protocol overhead: IFS, ACKs, backoff, retries, small frames. One active client on a clean radio can see about 50% of PHY as application throughput. A handful of clients drops that toward 45%. A busy room (a dozen or more active) is about 40%. Example: 30× 1-stream 11n clients at 72 Mbps PHY × 40% ≈ 29 Mbps total ≈ 950 kbps each — not enough for 2 Mbps unicast video. Same room, 2-stream 11ac at 173 Mbps × 40% ≈ 2.3 Mbps each. One 3-stream laptop left alone: 289 × 50% ≈ 145 Mbps. The math is boring on purpose.

What does “60% of the channel is yours” mean?

Wi-Fi is a shared half-duplex medium. Neighboring APs on the same or overlapping channel (CCI / ACI) burn airtime you do not get. Isolated is the classroom with no other BSS — 100% of the leftover airtime after protocol overhead. Typical assumes about 60% remains yours. Crowded is 40%. Those are knobs. Using one of them on every band is a story, not a floor. 2.4 GHz is often crowded when 5 and 6 are not. This is not a substitute for looking at channel utilization on a survey.

Why do extra SSIDs hurt?

Every SSID sends beacons, usually at the lowest basic rate (often 1 Mbps on 2.4, 6 Mbps on 5/6), plus probe responses. Four SSIDs is a lot. Eight is how you donate the 2.4 GHz radio to management frames. We subtract that tax before we talk about user traffic.

QAM, MCS, spatial streams — do I have to care?

Only if you want to. MCS is a gear number: modulation (QPSK, 16-QAM, 64-QAM, 256-QAM, 1024-QAM, 4096-QAM) plus a coding rate. Higher QAM packs more bits per symbol and needs a cleaner signal. Spatial streams are how many parallel radios the client actually has — most phones are 2SS, a pile of IoT is 1SS. We pick one MCS from link quality unless you override it, and we use that same rate up and down. Real cells rate-shift. The card names the radio that runs out first, then a modeled maximum and a smaller recommended count.

Does OFDMA / MU-MIMO 4× the number?

No. OFDMA helps when many clients send small frames at once. It does not turn a 20 MHz radio into four 20 MHz radios. MU-MIMO in the field has been underwhelming for the same reason most rooms are not a lab demo. We do not bake a marketing multiplier into the total.

Is this a coverage or channel plan?

No. It does not place APs, pick channels, or know your walls. If MCS is low with strong RSSI, that is interference, not distance. If MCS is low with weak RSSI, add an AP. Where the AP is mounted, and what the neighbors are doing right now, is a measurement. This tab will not walk the floor for you.

Why is the real answer “it depends”?

A Wi-Fi cell is a shared, contention-based radio, and most of the inputs are not on this form. People across the room do not hold one MCS. The rate shifts, and uplink often negotiates worse than downlink. 2.4, 5, and 6 GHz do not share interference, contention, or how far the signal goes, and 2.4 GHz is often just crowded. A real room is not one kind of device: generations, streams, widths, and apps share the air, and the slow devices spend more of it. A call can fail on delay, jitter, and loss while a stream still looks fine, because calls need both directions. Where you mount the AP, and what the neighbors are doing, changes the result. That belongs in a survey. This page compares stories. It does not settle the design.

Why isn’t there one speed for each person?

People on 2.4 GHz and people on 5 GHz are not in the same group. Averaging them hides the band that is already full. One device uses one radio and cannot add the others together. The card lists each band and names the one that runs out first.

Why is the recommended count lower than the model?

The modeled maximum fills this story and leaves no spare airtime. Wi-Fi does not run well full. The recommended count leaves the margin you picked unused. The default leaves 30% unused. Neither number is a headcount to put in a design.

Where do the data rates come from?

PHY rates follow IEEE 802.11 (n/ac/ax/be): channel width, streams, MCS/QAM, and guard interval. Device presets are common phones, laptops, and IoT. The AP list is a curated snapshot of internal-antenna Aruba campus and Juniper Mist models — radios and streams from public specs / Pathfinder hwspecs, not a live scrape. Scan radios are not client radios. Real speed is still less than the datasheet number.
