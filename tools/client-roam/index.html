    Glossary

    The words this tab uses, once. Nothing here changes the cartoon.

    RSSI, sticky, r/k/v, walls, and the rest
    RSSI
    How loud the AP is at the device, in dBm. Strong-and-stuck is a sticky client. Weak-and-stuck is a wall.
    Sticky client
    A device that holds the old AP into the next room. Typical, not broken. This tab is that device.
    Min basic rate
    The slowest rate the SSID will talk. Beacons use it. Raise it and the usable cell shrinks. The radio does not get quieter.
    802.11r / FT
    Fast BSS Transition. Shorter hole after the device has already decided. Does not pick the moment.
    802.11k
    Neighbor report. The client gets a list instead of scanning every channel. Faster discovery. Same trigger.
    802.11v / BTM
    BSS Transition Management: the network says “please go there.” This cartoon honors it. Real devices can shrug.
    dB vs dBm
    dBm is a level (RSSI, TX power). dB is a difference (a wall, hysteresis). A 14 dB wall is not “−14 dBm.”
    Wall loss
    Each wall crossed is 14 dB here. Next door is one. Across the hall is two. Doors are ignored on purpose.
    Hysteresis
    The new AP has to be enough better before the device bothers. Stops bouncing. Also makes sticky worse.
    Beacon
    “I am here,” sent at the lowest basic rate. If you cannot decode the beacon, you cannot join, even if RF energy is still on the floor.

    FAQ

    Does 802.11r make the client roam sooner?

    No. Check r and hit Play. The hole shrinks. The roam point does not move. A sticky client with FT still holds the old AP too long.

    Why does turning power up make roaming worse?

    Higher power makes cells bigger, not “better roaming.” The old AP stays loud farther into the next room, so the client waits longer. Overlap grows. Sticky gets worse.

    What does min basic rate actually change?

    The inner disc — the usable cell — not RF physics. Energy still goes the same distance. Raise the min rate and only the inner disc shrinks. The outer disc is RF still there.

    What do the two discs mean?

    Inner disc is the usable cell — the device can hold the min basic rate. Outer disc is RF still there. A sticky client can hold past the inner disc. 802.11v is what kicks it once it leaves that inner cell, if a neighbor can take it.

    What is a hard roam versus FT versus a drop?

    A hard roam drops the old AP and fully joins the next one — the radio goes dark in between. History shows that as one hop, not “left nobody.” FT (802.11r) is a fast handoff while still associated. A drop is off the radio with nobody to join yet.

    What do 802.11k and 802.11v actually do?

    802.11k gives the client a neighbor list, so it does not scan every channel — roam history will say it already knew the next AP. 802.11v is the serving AP telling the client to leave; the old AP flashes a kick. Neither one is a substitute for 802.11r’s short hole.

    Does 802.11r still work after the client drops?

    No. FT only applies while still associated. After a drop, the next join is a full 802.1X or PSK, even with r/k/v checked. Raise power or lower min rate so it can roam before it dies.

    Why is 802.1X the default?

    That is the long hole: EAP plus a 4-way handshake. PSK without r is already short. Fast roam looks magical on enterprise Wi-Fi because 802.1X without r is slow.

    Why isn’t the AP across the hall “right there”?

    Two walls. Each is about 14 dB here, so across the hall is 28 dB. Next door is one wall. That is the tax, not the hallway distance.

    Is this a coverage survey?

    No. It is a cartoon of one sticky device walking rooms and a hallway. Visualization and education only — not an RF survey, and not perfect RF.
